## Methodology notes

- `bt` through `bt5`: scored option trades from the underlying move (0.45 delta × move, minus $3/contract). Approximation, not real option fills.
- `bt6` onward: **real** 1-minute Alpaca option prices (actual trade prices, history back to March 2024), $0.01-0.02/leg slippage assumed unless noted. The $3 and $0.01-0.02 figures are bid/ask costs, not Robinhood commissions (Robinhood charges none).
- Standard discipline applied throughout: tune on one period, then look once at a locked/held-out period; check the tester itself with coin-flip trades; record failures, not just the best-looking variant.

### Variant-count / multiple-comparisons caveat (added 2026-11-14)

Several studies below searched many entry/exit variants and reported the best one. Reporting only the winner without saying how many were tried overstates confidence — a "best of 60" result is weaker evidence of a real edge than a single pre-specified test that happens to pass, even at the same headline profit factor. This is the same concern Alpaca's own "Elite-Bot" writeup flags with a deflated-Sharpe/trial-count correction; we don't have that correction built, but logging the count is the minimum fix. **Finding: the FP pattern James noticed is real and has an identifiable mechanism, not just a vibe.** Reading every FP trade row for Nov 12 (up day) through Nov 18 (down day):
- On Nov 12 (up day), FP's two **continuation** trades (trading *with* the day's direction) both hit target for +145 and +117 — the best single results in the whole sample. Its six **reversion** trades that day (betting price would pull back toward the 9:30 open) all lost, -23 to -72.
- On every day since Nov 13 (down), **continuation trades essentially stopped firing** — only one shows in the whole four-day stretch (Nov 17, -17) — and FP's book became almost entirely **reversion calls**: buying calls on the bet that price would revert *up* to the day's open. In a sustained downtrend, price doesn't revert up to the open — it keeps making new lows — so these reversion-call bets kept getting stopped out, repeatedly, same side, same mechanism, day after day (Nov 16: 6 of 7 trades were losing reversion calls; Nov 17: 5 of 6; Nov 18: 9 of 11, though two large winners partly offset that day).
- In plain terms: **FP's reversion leg is a bet against the prevailing trend, and its continuation leg — which would bet with the trend — almost never triggers on trending days.** The book is structurally biased toward "the market will snap back," which costs money for as long as a trend actually persists, and that is exactly what's happened for the last four sessions.
- Secondary oddity worth a code-level look, not yet explained at the time: several FP rows are tagged `"reason":"target"` (the underlying price target was reached) but still show a **negative** dollar P&L (e.g. Nov 18, QQQ, entryU 659.9 → target 661.08, pnl -46). Later resolved: not a bug, FP's targets can be smaller than the option's own spread + theta.

**Separate finding, not raised by James but surfaced by this review: CDR has never fired a single trade across all 8 days.** `cdrk.trades` and `cdrk.open` are empty every day, even though CDR is listed "active." Its first backtest (`bt16`) was run later the same day with a thin/inconsistent result; James chose to leave it running as-is.

## Lessons about the testing process itself

- Samples of 20 days and a few dozen trades produced several false edges; a full year overturned them.
- Trying many versions and keeping the best one inflates results — always tune on one period, then look once at a locked period.
- Always check the tester itself with coin-flip trades; one of ours (the trailing-exit tester) was optimistic by ~$1.40/trade.
- Win rate is the wrong target — quick-profit exits won most often and lost the most money overall.
- Changing rules repeatedly off small same-day samples (Nov 10: ~8 rule changes in one day) resets the sample each time and teaches nothing.
- A pattern that looks real in a small live sample (EMA "doing best on QQQ," ~64 trades) can vanish in a full backtest (`bt23`, 182 sessions) — always re-check a live-data impression against the fuller history before acting on it, when the data exists to do so.
- Free-tier option-chain data has real gaps for less-liquid strikes/expiries (seen in `bt19`-`bt21`'s error counts) — a high error count in a study is itself a finding, not just noise to ignore.
- Reporting the best of many tried variants, without saying how many were tried, overstates how much the result should be trusted (see variant-count caveat above, added 2026-11-14) — log the count and the full table for every new multi-variant study, not just the winner.
- A strategy sourced from a video transcript read by a session with no way to verify the video itself (`bt24`/IB) should be treated as a best-effort translation, not a confirmed one, until the mechanical rules are checked against the actual source again.
- `bt24` is a clean textbook case of the exact failure mode `bt5` and `bt23` already warned about: a thin, recent holdout window producing an eye-catching positive number (here, PF up to 8.3) that is really a handful of oversized winners (confirmed by the drop-best-5 check going negative) riding on top of a dev period that was already negative. The lesson generalizes: **always check dev before getting excited about holdout, and always run the drop-best-5 check before trusting a holdout profit factor above ~1.5 on a small sample.**
- **This same lesson applies to a live sample too, just in the other direction (2026-11-18):** James's live-data impression about FP (good in up markets, bad in down markets) held up under a direct look at the trade rows, with a clear mechanism (reversion-vs-trend bias).
